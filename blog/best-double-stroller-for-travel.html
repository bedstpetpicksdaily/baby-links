Best Double Stroller for Travel

Traveling with two little ones is a logistical puzzle, and the stroller you choose is the single biggest variable in whether your trip feels manageable or miserable. Airport gate checks, cobblestone streets, hotel elevators, and rental car trunks all conspire against the oversized double strollers that work beautifully at home. What you need is something that folds fast, weighs less than your carry-on, and still rolls smoothly when both kids fall asleep three miles into a museum day.

What to Look For

  • A fold that fits airline gate-check and overhead rules. Look for a folded footprint under roughly 22 x 16 x 12 inches if you're hoping to stow it overhead on a wide-body aircraft, and confirm the stroller collapses one-handed while you're holding a toddler. Anything requiring two hands and a knee is going to fail you at the jet bridge.
  • Total weight under 30 pounds — ideally under 20. You'll be lifting this stroller onto luggage carts, up subway stairs, and into overhead bins. Every pound matters when you're already carrying a diaper bag, two backpacks, and a stuffed rabbit that cannot be checked.
  • A narrow tandem or slim side-by-side profile. Standard doorways in older European hotels, restaurant aisles, and airport security lanes are unforgiving. Tandem (front-to-back) designs generally win here, but a side-by-side under 30 inches wide can also work if it fits through a standard 32-inch door.

Our Top Pick

The Zoe Twin+ (XL2) remains our recommendation for most traveling families. At just under 17 pounds for a full double, it's genuinely liftable with one arm, and it folds in about five seconds with a single pull strap — a detail that matters enormously when a gate agent is waving you forward. It's 30 inches wide, which clears standard doorways, and both seats recline nearly flat with independent canopies, so one kid can nap while the other watches the world.

What makes it specifically a travel stroller rather than just a light double: it comes with a padded carry bag and shoulder strap, the wheels are foam-filled so they'll never go flat in a country where you can't find a bike pump, and there are enough cup holders and parent pockets that you're not hauling a separate bag for water bottles and passports. It also accepts most infant car seats with adapters, which matters if you're traveling with a baby and a toddler rather than two walkers.

If you need a narrower profile — think Tokyo train stations or historic Italian towns — the Baby Jogger City Mini GT2 Double is wider but more rugged, and the Joovy Caboose UL tandem is the pick when your older child is mostly walking and only needs occasional rides.

Why This Works for This Situation

Travel punishes strollers in ways daily life doesn't. Baggage handlers will throw yours down a chute. You'll fold and unfold it fifteen times in a single day. You'll push it across airport carpet, then marble, then a gravel path to a beach. A stroller like the Zoe Twin+ succeeds because it was designed around the fold and the weight first, with comfort features layered on top — rather than being a full-size stroller that someone tried to make lighter. The quick one-pull fold means you're never the family holding up the boarding line, and the included carry bag means when you gate-check it, the frame and canopies arrive intact instead of scuffed and bent.

Just as important is what it does for your kids. Long travel days mean long stretches in the seat, and a stroller that can't recline turns nap time into a meltdown. Independent recline and independent canopies let you manage two children on completely different schedules — the four-year-old snacking upright and the eighteen-month-old asleep and shaded. Combined with a decent sun canopy rating and mesh panels for hot destinations, that flexibility is what keeps a travel day from unraveling at 2 p.m.

What to Avoid

  • Full-size double strollers with air-filled tires. They're wonderful on trails at home and terrible on planes. Air pressure changes in the cargo hold can cause blowouts, they weigh 35 to 40 pounds, and the folded size is often too large to gate-check on smaller regional aircraft.
  • Travel systems that require you to bring both car seats and bases. Unless you're driving, hauling two infant car seat bases through an airport is a losing game. Rent car seats at your destination or use a lightweight travel car seat, and bring a stroller that works standalone.
  • Any stroller with a two-step or multi-latch fold. Test the fold in your living room while holding a laundry basket. If you can't do it in under ten seconds with one hand occupied, it will fail you at TSA, at the gate, and at every taxi curb on your trip.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the lightest double stroller for travel?

The lightest double strollers typically weigh between 15-20 lbs and include models like the Zoe XL2 and Summer Infant 3D Lite Double, making them ideal for air travel and maneuvering through airports. Look for umbrella-style doubles or compact side-by-side designs, as tandem strollers tend to be heavier and harder to navigate in tight spaces.

Can you take a double stroller on an airplane?

Yes, most airlines allow you to gate-check double strollers at no additional cost, similar to single strollers, or you can collapse it and check it with luggage. Lightweight, compact models under 20 lbs that fold small (like the Mountain Buggy Nano Duo) are your best bet for hassle-free air travel.

What size double stroller fits in a car trunk?

A compact double stroller should fold to roughly 30-35 inches long and 25-30 inches wide to fit most car trunks comfortably. Umbrella-style doubles and narrow tandem strollers are the most trunk-friendly options, whereas full-size side-by-side doubles may require an SUV or minivan.

Is a tandem or side-by-side double stroller better for travel?

Tandem strollers are generally better for travel since they're narrower, easier to fit through doorways, and more maneuverable in crowds and airports, though they're often longer. Side-by-side strollers offer more comfort and equal visibility for both kids but are wider and bulkier, making them less practical for travel unless you have an SUV.
