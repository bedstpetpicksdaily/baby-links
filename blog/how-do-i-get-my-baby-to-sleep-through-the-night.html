How Do I Get My Baby to Sleep Through the Night?

Most babies can sleep 6–8 hour stretches by 3–6 months old, and the fastest way to get there is a consistent bedtime routine, a dark and cool sleep environment, and putting your baby down drowsy but awake so they learn to fall asleep independently. That last piece — self-soothing — is the single biggest predictor of whether a baby sleeps through the night, because every human wakes briefly between sleep cycles and babies who can't resettle themselves will call for you every single time.

The Short Answer

Establish a predictable 20–30 minute bedtime routine (bath, pajamas, feed, book, song, bed) and run it at the same time every night. Put your baby into the crib awake so they learn to fall asleep without being rocked, fed, or held to sleep. Keep the room dark (blackout curtains), cool (68–72°F), and add white noise to mask household sounds. Then give it two to three weeks of consistency — most families see meaningful improvement in that window, and full night sleep typically arrives between 4 and 6 months once night feeds are no longer nutritionally necessary.

The Full Explanation

"Sleeping through the night" is a misleading phrase. Nobody sleeps straight through — adults included. We cycle through light and deep sleep every 90 minutes or so and surface briefly at each transition. Adults flip the pillow and go back under without remembering it. Babies do the same thing, but if the only way they know how to fall asleep is nursing, rocking, or being held, they'll need that exact condition recreated at 1 a.m., 3 a.m., and 5 a.m. This is called a sleep association, and it's the root cause of most night waking in otherwise healthy babies.

Age matters enormously. Newborns (0–3 months) physically cannot sleep through the night. Their stomachs are tiny, their circadian rhythm isn't developed, and they need to eat every 2–4 hours. Trying to sleep-train a newborn is futile and can interfere with weight gain. From around 4 months, babies develop adult-like sleep architecture — this is the infamous "4-month sleep regression," which is actually a permanent maturation, not a phase that passes. It's also the point where teaching independent sleep becomes both possible and worthwhile.

Daytime affects nighttime. An overtired baby sleeps worse, not better. Cortisol spikes when a baby is kept awake too long, making it harder to fall asleep and causing more frequent waking. Watch wake windows: roughly 45–60 minutes for newborns, 2–3 hours at 4–6 months, 3–4 hours at 9–12 months. Capping late-afternoon naps and making sure daytime feeds are full and unhurried also reduces the drive to make up calories overnight.

The sleep environment is non-negotiable. Light suppresses melatonin, so blackout curtains matter more than parents expect — especially in summer. White noise at a steady 50–60 decibels masks the door clicks and dog barks that cause partial arousals. Room temperature should sit between 68 and 72°F; overheating is both a sleep disruptor and a SIDS risk factor. Follow safe sleep rules throughout: firm flat mattress, fitted sheet only, baby on their back, no bumpers, blankets, pillows, or stuffed animals.

Night weaning is a separate step. Many babies over 6 months who are gaining weight well no longer need calories overnight, but habit persists. If your pediatrician confirms your baby is ready, you can gradually reduce night feeds — shortening nursing sessions by a minute or two each night, or reducing bottle volume by half an ounce every few nights — rather than cutting them abruptly.

Choose a method and commit. Options range from gradual (chair method, pick-up/put-down, gentle fading of your involvement) to more direct (timed check-ins, sometimes called Ferber). Research consistently shows all of them work when applied consistently, and none show harm to attachment or long-term emotional health. The method matters far less than the consistency. Switching approaches every three nights teaches your baby that persistence gets results, which is the opposite of what you want.

What the Experts Say

The American Academy of Pediatrics notes that most infants are developmentally capable of sleeping through the night by 6 months, and encourages putting babies down drowsy but awake starting around 2–4 months to build self-soothing skills. The AAP also recommends room-sharing (baby in their own crib or bassinet in your room) for at least the first 6 months to reduce SIDS risk — though many sleep consultants observe that babies often sleep better once moved to their own room after that point, since they're less likely to be roused by parental noise and movement.

Pediatric sleep researchers, including the widely cited work of Dr. Jodi Mindell at Children's Hospital of Philadelphia, have found that a consistent bedtime routine alone — with no other intervention — significantly reduces night wakings and shortens the time it takes babies to fall asleep, with effects showing up within two weeks. Multiple randomized trials of behavioral sleep interventions have found no negative effects on infant stress hormones, attachment security, or parent-child bonding at follow-up, while maternal depression scores consistently improve.

Every credible expert adds the same caveat: rule out medical causes first. Reflux, food sensitivities, ear infections, sleep apnea, and eczema all cause night waking that no amount of sleep training will fix. If your baby's sleep suddenly worsens or they seem uncomfortable, call your pediatrician before assuming it's behavioral.

The Product Solution

The hardest part of teaching independent sleep is knowing when to go in. Every squawk over a basic audio monitor sounds like distress, so parents rush in, fully wake the baby, and reset the whole process. A video baby monitor solves this by letting you actually see what's happening — and most of the time what's happening is a baby with their eyes closed, fussing through a sleep cycle transition, who will resettle in 90 seconds if left alone.

When shopping, prioritize a few specific features. Night vision is essential, since you'll be watching in a pitch-black room. Two-way audio lets you offer a reassuring word without opening the door. Pan-tilt-zoom means you can check on a mobile baby who's rolled to the far corner of the crib. Room temperature display helps you hit that 68–72°F target without guessing. And decide between a dedicated-monitor model — a standalone parent unit with a closed radio connection, no Wi-Fi, no app — or a Wi-Fi model that streams to your phone from anywhere. Dedicated units are more reliable and more private; Wi-Fi units are more convenient if you travel or want a caregiver to check in.

A video monitor also makes room-sharing transitions easier, helps you spot patterns (is she waking at the same time every night? that's often a habit, not hunger), and gives anxious parents the reassurance to stay in bed instead of hovering — which, practically speaking, is what actually lets babies learn to sleep.

Quick Tips

  • Put baby down awake. Drowsy but conscious. This one habit does more for night sleep than anything else on this list.
  • Pause before you respond. Wait 3–5 minutes before entering the room. A large share of night "wakings" resolve on their own.
  • Protect the environment. Blackout curtains, white noise, 68–72°F, and a bare crib — every night, including travel nights.
  • Pick one method and give it 14 days. Consistency beats technique. Changing approaches mid-stream is the most common reason sleep training fails.

The Bottom Line

Getting your baby to sleep through the night comes down to three things: a consistent routine, a sleep environment engineered for darkness and quiet, and the space to practice falling asleep without you. Be patient with the timeline, rule out medical issues if progress stalls, and remember that nearly every baby gets there — usually within a few weeks of consistent effort.

Frequently Asked Questions

At what age can babies sleep through the night?

Most babies can physically sleep through the night (6-8 hours) between 3-6 months old, but many aren't developmentally ready until 6 months or later. Newborns under 3 months need to feed every 2-4 hours, so expecting them to sleep through is unrealistic. Talk to your pediatrician about when your baby is ready based on their weight and feeding patterns.

Why does my baby wake up so many times at night?

Nighttime wakings can be caused by hunger, discomfort (wet diaper, too hot/cold), developmental milestones, teething, or sleep associations like needing to be rocked. If your baby is over 6 months and still waking frequently after ruling out hunger and discomfort, it may be a sleep habit issue that can be addressed with gentle sleep training methods.

What's the best sleep schedule to help my baby sleep through the night?

Babies sleep better with a consistent bedtime routine and schedule, typically going down between 7-9 PM. Ensure they're getting enough daytime naps and total sleep (14-17 hours for infants), and avoid bedtime too late, which can cause overtiredness and frequent night wakings. A predictable routine signals to their body it's time to sleep and helps regulate their natural sleep-wake cycle.

Should I use sleep training to get my baby to sleep through the night?

Sleep training can be effective for babies 6 months and older who are developmentally ready, but it's not necessary for all families. Popular methods include cry-it-out, Ferber method, and gentler approaches like pick-up/put-down—choose one aligned with your parenting style and comfort level. Always consult your pediatrician first to rule out underlying issues like reflux, allergies, or hunger.
