PECS (Pyramid Educational Consultants): The Research-Backed Picture Card Route

PECS — the Picture Exchange Communication System — is the most studied low-tech option available, with a formal six-phase protocol developed by Bondy and Frost. It is not just "picture cards"; it teaches the child to physically hand a card to a communication partner, which builds the crucial concept of initiating with another person. That distinction matters. Many children learn to press a talking button as a cause-and-effect game without ever addressing a human being; PECS structurally prevents that. Costs vary: a laminated starter symbol set and binder can be assembled for $30–$80, the official PECS Training Manual runs around $90–$110, and a two-day Level 1 workshop for parents or staff is typically $400+ per person. That training is the hidden cost, and skipping it is why a lot of home PECS binders end up in a closet. Downsides: no voice output, so the child can't call across a room or get a stranger's attention; binders get lost, wet, and chewed; and vocabulary expansion means printing, cutting, and laminating forever. It is also unhelpful for children with significant fine-motor or visual impairments.

Attainment Company GoTalk: Purpose-Built Mid-Tech Devices

The GoTalk family (GoTalk 4+, 9+, 20+, and the GoTalk Express) is what many school districts hand out, and for good reason. A GoTalk 9+ typically retails around $230–$280, giving you nine message cells across five recordable levels — 45 messages total — plus core buttons that stay constant across levels. Compared to a Joyrealtoys board, you are paying roughly 5–8x more and getting: a genuinely rugged sealed keyguard-compatible housing, level switching so vocabulary can grow with context (breakfast page, playground page, therapy page), a battery that lasts months, and overlay software that produces professional symbol grids. Attainment also supports these devices for years and sells replacement parts, which matters when a device is your child's mouth. The trade-offs are that recording 45 messages by hand is tedious, the audio quality is functional rather than crisp, and there is no text-to-speech or literacy pathway — a GoTalk cannot grow into sentence generation the way a tablet app can.

Frequently Asked Questions

Will using picture cards or an AAC device stop my child from talking?

No. This is the most persistent myth in the field, and the research consistently contradicts it. Multiple reviews of AAC intervention studies, including Millar and colleagues' widely cited analysis, found that AAC use was associated with gains in speech production for the majority of participants and speech loss in essentially none. Giving a child a reliable way to be understood reduces frustration and typically increases vocalization attempts rather than replacing them.

Is a cheap recordable button like Joyrealtoys' actually 'real' AAC?

It's a legitimate low-tech AAC tool, but it is not a robust AAC system. Single-message and few-cell recordable devices are widely used by SLPs for high-frequency requests, participation in routines, and testing whether a child grasps voice output. What they can't do is provide the hundreds of core words a child needs to say anything other than pre-recorded phrases. Use them as a supplement or a starting point, not as the destination.

Can I get an AAC device covered by insurance or my school district?

Often, yes — but only for dedicated speech-generating devices or app-plus-device systems prescribed after a formal AAC evaluation by a licensed SLP. In the US, Medicaid and most private plans cover SGDs under durable medical equipment when medical necessity is documented, and IDEA requires school districts to provide AAC when it's written into an IEP. Toy-grade recordable boards and DIY PECS binders are almost never reimbursed, which is exactly why they're worth using as a stopgap while paperwork moves.
