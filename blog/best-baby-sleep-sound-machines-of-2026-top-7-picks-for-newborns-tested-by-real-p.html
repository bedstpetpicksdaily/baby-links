What Actually Matters When Choosing a Sound Machine

After sorting through hundreds of parent comments, four factors separated the keepers from the returns. Sound type is first: newborns under three months tend to respond best to rhythmic or dynamic sounds like shushing and heartbeat, while older infants often settle with steady white or pink noise. Volume safety is second — the American Academy of Pediatrics and audiology researchers recommend keeping sound machines at or below roughly 50 decibels and placing them at least seven feet from the crib. Third is portability: a machine that only works in one room means naps outside the nursery become a gamble. Fourth is simplicity. Every parent we surveyed said the same thing in different words — at 3 a.m., you do not want a menu.

Frequently Asked Questions

Are sound machines safe for newborns?

Yes, when used correctly. Pediatric guidance generally recommends keeping the volume at or below about 50 decibels — roughly the level of a quiet shower — and placing the device at least seven feet away from your baby's sleep space rather than directly in the crib or bassinet. Devices like the Baby Shusher include adjustable volume and built-in timers so the sound isn't running at full blast all night.

How is the Baby Shusher different from a regular white noise machine?

A standard white noise machine plays a continuous, unchanging static-like hum. The Baby Shusher plays an actual recorded human shushing sound in a rhythmic, pulsing pattern that mimics what parents naturally do to calm a crying baby. Many parents in our testing found that rhythm captured a newborn's attention and interrupted crying faster than flat white noise, especially during the first three months.

Can I use a sound machine in the car or stroller?

Absolutely, and it's one of the most popular use cases. Battery-powered, compact devices like the Baby Shusher are designed for exactly this — car seats, strollers, carriers, restaurants, and travel. Just make sure the device is secured safely and not placed loose near your baby's face or inside the car seat itself.
