What Is the Best Stroller for a Newborn?

The best stroller for a newborn is one that allows your baby to lie completely flat or accepts an infant car seat — which means a travel system, a full-size stroller with a bassinet attachment, or a stroller with a fully reclining seat. Newborns lack the neck and trunk strength to sit upright safely, so flat or near-flat positioning (150 degrees or more of recline) isn't a luxury feature; it's a safety requirement for the first several months.

The Short Answer

For most families, a travel system stroller is the best choice for a newborn because it clicks directly with an infant car seat, letting you move a sleeping baby from car to sidewalk without unbuckling them. If you'd rather skip the car seat click-in, look for a stroller with a bassinet attachment or a seat that reclines fully flat. Either option keeps your newborn's airway open and spine supported. Avoid lightweight umbrella strollers and most jogging strollers until your baby can sit up unassisted, usually around six months.

The Full Explanation

Newborn stroller safety comes down to one issue: head position. An infant who slumps forward with their chin on their chest can develop positional asphyxia, a restriction of the airway that can happen silently. That's why every major stroller safety recommendation for babies under six months centers on flat or heavily reclined positioning.

There are three ways a stroller can safely accommodate a newborn:

  • Infant car seat compatibility (travel system): The car seat clicks into the stroller frame using adapters or a built-in mount. The car seat itself is designed for newborns from day one, so the stroller simply becomes the wheels. This is the most common and most convenient approach.
  • Bassinet attachment: A rigid, flat-bottomed carrycot that snaps onto the stroller frame. Bassinets offer the best sleeping position and the most airflow, and many are approved for overnight sleep. They're standard on European-style strollers and usually sold separately on American models.
  • Full-recline seat: Some stroller seats lay back to roughly 165–180 degrees, with a footrest that lifts to close the leg gap. Check the manufacturer's minimum age — many say "birth" only when used in the fully flat position.

Beyond recline, a few other features matter more in the newborn stage than people expect. Suspension and larger wheels smooth out sidewalk cracks, which matters because newborn heads have almost no shock absorption. A deep, extendable canopy protects skin that's too sensitive for sunscreen in the first six months. A one-handed fold is genuinely important when you're holding a baby in a parking lot. And a storage basket you can actually reach saves you from hanging a diaper bag on the handlebar, which is the leading cause of stroller tip-overs.

What doesn't matter as much as marketing suggests: weight. Parents obsess over stroller weight, but a 22-pound stroller that folds in one motion is easier to live with than a 15-pound stroller that requires two hands and a knee. Test the fold before you test the scale.

What the Experts Say

Pediatric guidance from the American Academy of Pediatrics is consistent on this point: infants should be positioned so their airway stays open, and they should not be left to sleep in a semi-upright car seat for extended periods. The AAP recommends moving a sleeping infant from a car seat to a flat surface once you reach your destination — which is an argument in favor of strollers with bassinets for long outings, and an argument against using the car seat as a default all-day carrier.

Car seat manufacturers and child passenger safety technicians generally advise limiting continuous car seat time to about two hours. That doesn't make travel systems unsafe — it makes them ideal for errands, appointments, and short trips, which is exactly what most newborn outings are. For a two-hour walk in the park, a bassinet is the better tool.

Certified Child Passenger Safety Technicians also stress that you should never use aftermarket adapters or third-party parts to force a car seat onto a stroller it wasn't designed for. Only use adapters made or explicitly approved by the stroller or car seat brand. Matched travel systems eliminate this problem entirely, which is part of their appeal.

The Product Solution

A travel system stroller solves the newborn problem in a single purchase: you get an infant car seat, a base for your vehicle, and a stroller frame that the car seat clicks into, all designed and tested together. There's no adapter guesswork, no compatibility research, and no risk of buying a stroller only to discover your car seat brand isn't supported.

The practical advantage shows up immediately. Your newborn falls asleep in the car on the way to a doctor's appointment — you unclip the car seat, click it into the stroller frame, and walk in without ever waking them. Later, when your baby can sit up, you remove the car seat and use the stroller's own seat, which typically carries through to 40 or 50 pounds. That's three to four years of use from one system.

When comparing options, prioritize: a car seat with a low minimum weight (4–5 pounds) if you're expecting a smaller baby, a stroller seat that reverses to face you, a canopy with UPF-rated fabric, and a fold that stands on its own. Quick Tips

  • Test the recline in the store, not the box. Push the seat all the way back and look at the angle — if it stops well short of flat and there's no bassinet or car seat option, it's not a newborn stroller.
  • Practice the fold before your due date. Set it up, fold it, and load it into your trunk at least three times while you're not holding a baby. You'll discover any deal-breakers while returns are still easy.
  • Never hang bags from the handlebar. Use the under-seat basket. Handlebar weight is the number one cause of strollers tipping backward, especially when the car seat is mounted high on the frame.
  • Skip the umbrella stroller until six months. They're light and cheap, but almost none recline far enough for a newborn's airway, and the fabric seats offer no head or trunk support.

Conclusion

The best newborn stroller is simply one that lets your baby lie flat or ride safely in an infant car seat — everything else is preference. Choose a travel system for maximum convenience or a bassinet-compatible stroller for the best sleep positioning, and you'll have the first six months covered.

Frequently Asked Questions

What features should a newborn stroller have?

A newborn stroller should have a fully reclinable seat that goes completely flat, since newborns can't sit upright yet. Look for good suspension, a sturdy frame, and a large canopy for sun protection, plus adequate storage for diaper bags and essentials.

Can you use a regular stroller for a newborn?

Most regular strollers aren't suitable for newborns because they don't recline fully flat, which can compromise breathing and spinal development. You'll either need a stroller with a bassinet attachment, a travel system with an infant car seat, or a dedicated newborn stroller.

Is a travel system or standalone stroller better for newborns?

A travel system (stroller + car seat combo) is more convenient since you can click the infant car seat directly onto the stroller frame without waking your baby. However, standalone strollers with flat recline are lighter and often easier to maneuver once your baby outgrows the car seat phase.

How long can a newborn stay in a stroller?

Newborns can stay in a stroller for short trips, but shouldn't be in a reclined position longer than 1-2 hours at a time due to breathing concerns. For longer outings, take breaks and have your baby in an upright position or lay them flat on their back in a safe sleep space.
